# Project Charter (pm-030)

## What It Does

Produces a formally authorized project charter that officially launches the project, defines its purpose and scope, establishes governance, and secures sign-off from the sponsor and key stakeholders.

## When to Use

- After feasibility study returns a "Go" decision
- When there is a clear business problem or opportunity to address
- Before any significant project expenditure or team mobilization
- When organizational governance requires a formal document before work begins

## Inputs

- Feasibility study results
- Business case or strategic alignment brief
- Initial scope statement
- Identified sponsor and key stakeholders
- High-level timeline and budget range
- Constraints and assumptions list

## Workflow

1. **Draft purpose and business context** — Articulate the business problem, opportunity, or regulatory driver. Link to strategic objectives. One to two paragraphs maximum.
2. **Define scope boundaries** — State what is in scope and explicitly what is out of scope. Use bullet points. Ambiguity here is the #1 source of scope creep later.
3. **Identify key deliverables** — List the 5–10 major tangible outcomes the project will produce. Be specific and measurable where possible.
4. **Establish governance structure** — Define decision authority (Steering Committee, sponsor, PM), escalation path, and approval requirements. Include meeting cadence if known.
5. **Assign roles** — Name the sponsor, project manager, core team leads, and key stakeholder representatives. Include their responsibilities in one sentence each.
6. **Set high-level schedule and budget** — Provide not-to-exceed budget and key milestone dates. Use ranges if figures are estimates; refine in planning phase.
7. **Document constraints and assumptions** — List known limitations (budget, timeline, technology, resource availability) and the key assumptions being made.
8. **Formal sign-off** — Present charter to sponsor and key stakeholders. Obtain explicit sign-off (email or wet signature). File in project repository.
9. **Distribute** — Share the approved charter with all core team members and post to project collaboration space.

## Output

- Signed project charter (1–3 pages)
- Distribution list confirming who received the charter
- Project repository or folder setup with charter as the first artifact

## Tips

- A charter without sign-off is not a charter — it is a draft. Get the signatures first.
- Scope and out-of-scope are the two most important sections; write them with a stakeholder who will hold you accountable.
- If the sponsor refuses to sign, the project is not authorized — do not proceed.
- Budget and schedule in the charter are Authorization thresholds, not commitments. Refine them in the planning phase.
- Include a "what happens if we stop" clause — it clarifies the cost of inaction and strengthens sponsor commitment.
